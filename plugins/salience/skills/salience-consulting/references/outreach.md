# Outreach

The standard: **it should be worth receiving even if it goes nowhere.**

Executive advisory work is sold through credibility and relationships. Sequenced volume outreach
works for products and actively damages an executive brand, because the recipients are the exact
people whose opinion the brand depends on.

## Warm paths first

Always check the relationship record before drafting anything cold. A shared contact who will make
an introduction outperforms the best cold message by a wide margin, and the connection frequently
already exists.

```
Before I draft this - you already know two people connected to Meridian. Tom Alvarez
worked with their VP Product, and Dana Whitfield is a former colleague of the CEO.
An introduction from either beats anything I can write cold. Want me to draft the
ask to Tom instead?
```

## Research

Assemble only what informs a real conversation. Public sources only.

- What the company does and its current situation
- Public signals of the relevant problem — hiring patterns, funding, leadership changes, product moves
- Who owns the problem, and who signs
- Any genuine connection
- What the user could say that is useful before any engagement exists

**If there is no genuine reason to reach out, say so.** Manufacturing a pretext is what makes
outreach read as outreach.

## Message construction

```
[Why them, specifically]   An observation about their situation showing real attention.
                           Not a compliment on their post.
[Why you, briefly]         One line of relevance. Not a résumé.
[The small ask]            A conversation. Easy to decline.
```

Under 150 words. Never opens with the ask.

### Worked example

> Saw you've hired three demand-gen people in five months and posted for a marketing ops lead
> twice. That combination usually means the funnel is producing volume the team can't measure, and
> the instinct is to add people to the part that's visible.
>
> I run the measurement side of this for B2B SaaS at your stage — most recently at Hollis, where
> the same pattern turned out to be an attribution problem rather than a capacity one.
>
> Worth twenty minutes? Happy to tell you what I'd look at first, whether or not it goes anywhere.

Specific to observable facts, offers a hypothesis with something at stake, brief relevance, small
ask, easy exit.

### Acknowledging a gap

Where there is an obvious mismatch the recipient will notice anyway — scope, sector, a stated
requirement — name it early and redirect once. Confident acknowledgment reads as senior; hoping it
goes unmentioned reads as evasive, and it will come up regardless.

> "I haven't run EMEA, which I know is in the brief. What I have run is three business units with
> different buying motions, and the transferable part is the operating model rather than the
> geography. Happy to be wrong about that."

One sentence, then move on. Do not litigate it.

### What fails

- "I came across your profile and was impressed by your background"
- A compliment on a recent post as the opener
- Leading with credentials
- Any message that would work unchanged for a hundred recipients
- An ask for a referral in the first message
- "Just following up" as the entire second message

## Follow-up

**One follow-up, then stop.** The second message is the last one. Sequences of four and five are for
products, and at executive level they are remembered.

The follow-up must add something — a relevant piece of information, a change in their situation, a
narrower ask. "Bumping this to the top of your inbox" adds nothing and signals that the first
message was volume.

> "Saw the Series C announcement — congratulations. Makes the measurement question more urgent
> rather than less, in my experience. Offer of twenty minutes stands; if the timing's wrong, no
> need to reply."

Give a graceful exit explicitly — "no worries if the timing is wrong" or "happy if the answer is
no". It costs nothing and it is the difference between a message that can be declined and one that
has to be avoided.

Then stop. If a second message goes unanswered, they are not interested. Silence is an answer, and
respecting it is what makes a later approach possible.

Every message must pass the screenshot test: would the user be comfortable if this were posted
publicly by the recipient?

## Refused

Salience will not produce or operate:

- Bulk or templated messaging to lists
- Automated multi-step sequences
- Bulk connection requests
- A pitch inside a connection request
- Outreach misrepresenting how the user found the recipient
- Fabricated common ground

Every message requires approval before sending, individually. There is no batch approval — see
`../../salience-governance/SKILL.md`.

## Inbound is the better system

When the user asks how to get more consulting work, check the inbound system before recommending
outreach: is the profile positioned for the offer, does Featured carry a diagnostic and a path, is
there content demonstrating the judgment being bought, is there visible proof?

Outbound compensates for a weak inbound system. Fixing the system is usually higher leverage, and
saying so is usually the honest answer.
