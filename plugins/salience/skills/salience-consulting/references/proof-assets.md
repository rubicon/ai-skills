# Proof Assets

Case studies and testimonials. The strongest material an advisory practice has, and the material
almost nobody makes time to create — the engagement ends, the client is happy, and six months later
there is a sales conversation with nothing to show.

## The hero principle

**The client is the hero. The user is the guide.**

This single reframing is what separates proof from bragging, and executives get it wrong more often
than anyone because they are used to writing performance reviews about themselves.

| Wrong | Right |
|---|---|
| "I built a demand engine that generated $6M in pipeline." | "They went from a pipeline nobody trusted to a forecast the board stopped arguing about — in two quarters." |
| "I consolidated their MarTech stack." | "Their team stopped spending three weeks a month assembling a report that was wrong when it arrived." |

Write what the **client experienced**, not what the user delivered. The user's contribution is
visible in the outcome without being asserted.

## Before drafting: the gate

Do not draft a case study without **at least one specific number**. If the user says "it went
well", stop and ask:

> "I need one number for this to be credible. Any of these works, and a rough recollection is fine:
> revenue or pipeline change, time saved, cost removed, a rate that moved (conversion, retention,
> win rate), or a volume that changed."

What the user recalls is `stated` and usable. What a document shows is `verified`. What Salience
calculates is neither, and never ships — see the evidence contract.

## Result strength governs language strength

Evidence tier decides *whether* a claim ships. This decides *how confidently it is phrased*.
Overclaiming on thin evidence is the fastest way a strong case study becomes a liability.

| Result type | Example | Phrase it as |
|---|---|---|
| **Hard metric** | Blended CAC down 34% | State it flatly. No hedge |
| **Soft but real** | Three teams finally using one definition of a qualified lead | "For the first time…" — narrative, not numeric |
| **Proxy** | The round closed two months later | "Contributed to…" — never "caused" |
| **Directional** | Reporting got noticeably faster | Name the change, not a magnitude. Never invent one |

The temptation is always to promote a proxy into a cause. A case study claiming an engagement
caused a funding round will be tested by the first sophisticated buyer who reads it.

## The tension check

A case study with no difficulty is a list, not a story, and it reads as a brochure. Before drafting,
answer: **what made this hard?** A constraint, a disagreement, something that failed first, a
deadline, a person who had to be convinced.

If the honest answer is "nothing, it was straightforward", say so and write the two-liner rather
than the narrative. Not every engagement is a case study.

## Three formats from one engagement

| Format | Length | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Two-liner** | 2 sentences | Profile About, proposals, a bio, an introduction |
| **Story** | 150–250 words | A LinkedIn post, the opening of a sales conversation |
| **Full** | Long-form | Featured, a site, an RFP response |

**Story shape:** the situation and what was at stake · what made it hard · what was actually done ·
what changed, with the number.

## Confidentiality

Everything in `../../salience-content/SKILL.md` under client work applies, and matters more here
because a case study is more detailed than a post.

- Confirm publication rights before drafting, not after
- Default to an anonymized descriptor — "a $60M B2B SaaS company, PE-backed"
- Never publish a client metric the client has not published
- Never make a former client identifiable by accumulation — industry plus size plus timeline plus
  city identifies one company even with no name attached

Where permission is uncertain, write the anonymized version and record the gap.

## Testimonials

### Asking

Give the person a genuine choice, and make the low-effort path real:

```
Hi [Name],

Working on [specific thing] with you was one of the more interesting problems I've had
lately — particularly [specific detail].

Would you be open to a short recommendation I could use?

Either works:
  A) I send you three questions and you answer in a few lines
  B) I draft two or three bullets you edit, ignore, or rewrite

Both take about a minute. Happy to write yours first either way.
```

### The three questions

1. What was the situation before we worked together?
2. What changed?
3. Would you recommend this to someone else, and why?

These produce usable material because they ask for narrative rather than praise. "What was it like
working with me" produces adjectives; "what was the situation before" produces a story.

### Quality

A testimonial is worth using when it has a **specific outcome**, sounds like a person rather than
marketing copy, and does not lead with praise. If what comes back is warm but empty, one gentle
follow-up is reasonable:

> "Thank you — genuinely. One small ask: if you can name a number, even roughly, it makes this far
> more useful to the next person deciding. Entirely optional."

If the second attempt still yields nothing specific, use it as-is in the short format and stop
asking. A vague testimonial from a credible name still carries weight; a third request costs the
relationship more than the testimonial is worth.

### The boundary

Drafting bullets a person then edits and submits is normal professional courtesy.

**Writing testimony as the client, for them to paste unchanged, is fabricated social proof and
Salience will not produce it** — see `../../salience-governance/SKILL.md`.

Every draft-on-behalf must pass one check: **does this contain any claim the client did not make,
or any number the user added?** If yes, it is not a draft of their words, it is an invention with
their name on it.
