# Offer Design

"Open to consulting opportunities" is not an offer and produces no inbound. An offer names a
problem, a shape, and a boundary.

## The five elements

1. **The problem**, as the buyer states it
2. **The engagement shape** — fractional, advisory, project, board, speaking
3. **Duration and commitment**, realistically
4. **What is explicitly out of scope** — the most useful and most skipped element
5. **What the buyer gets** — outcomes, not activities

## Shapes

| Shape | Buyer | Commitment | Sold on | Fails when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fractional executive | No budget or need for a full-time seat | 1-2 days/week, 6-12 months | Operating capability | Sold as execution capacity at one day a week |
| Advisory | Founder or CEO needing periodic counsel | Hours monthly | Judgment and access | The buyer actually wanted an operator |
| Project | A bounded problem | Weeks, defined deliverable | A defined outcome | Scope was never bounded |
| Board / committee | Governance need | Quarterly | Standing and experience | Treated as a consulting foothold |
| Speaking | Event or internal audience | One-off | Point of view | Used as a disguised sales pitch |

Each has a different buyer and a different motion. One message selling all five sells none.

## Scope boundaries

The out-of-scope list is what makes an engagement succeed, because it is where expectations
actually break.

```
Fractional CMO - what this is and isn't

In scope
  Marketing strategy and the operating model
  Team structure, hiring, and coaching the leads
  Measurement architecture and the reporting the board sees
  Vendor and agency selection and management
  Board-level marketing communication

Not in scope
  Day-to-day execution - campaign build, copy production, design
  Being reachable same-day. I work Tuesdays and Thursdays
  Sales management, even where the line is blurry
  Fixing a product problem with marketing

Commitment
  Two days a week, six-month minimum, reviewed at three
```

The "not in scope" section is what a good buyer reads most carefully. It signals someone who has
done this before and had it go wrong.

## Pricing posture

Salience does not set prices. It helps the user reason:

- **Price on the value of the problem**, not on hours, wherever the shape allows
- **A low retainer anchors permanently** — including with future clients who compare notes
- **Discounting the first engagement sets the ceiling.** Prefer reducing scope over reducing rate
- **Name the range and stop talking.** The silence after a number is the buyer's to fill
- **A minimum commitment protects both sides.** A fractional engagement under three months cannot
  produce the outcome it promises

Where market data is genuinely unknown, say so rather than inventing a benchmark.

## Ideal client

Specific enough to disqualify. Stage, size, model, ownership, the situation creating the need, who
signs — and **who is not a fit.**

The disqualification list is what makes referrals work. A network can only send the right people if
it knows which people are wrong. It also prevents the most expensive outcome in advisory work: a
bad-fit engagement that consumes a quarter and produces a reference the user cannot use.

```
Good fit
  B2B SaaS, $20M-$200M ARR, PE-backed or post-Series-C
  Growth has flattened and nobody can explain why
  A CEO who wants the number, not the narrative
  Existing marketing team of 5-25 to work through

Not a fit
  Pre-product-market-fit
  Looking for execution capacity - hire an agency
  Marketing reporting into Sales
  Wants a name on the website, not an operator
```
