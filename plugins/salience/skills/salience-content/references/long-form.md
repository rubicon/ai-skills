# Long-Form

Articles and newsletters. Lower reach, longer half-life, and the formats that most improve
retrievability in AI search — see `../../salience-profile/references/ai-visibility.md`.

## When long-form is right

- The argument genuinely needs more than 1,300 characters
- The piece should be citable and findable a year from now
- It is the substantive artifact behind a Featured slot
- The user wants an owned audience rather than a rented one

## When it is not

- The idea fits in a post. Padding a post into an article is visible
- The user will not sustain it. One abandoned newsletter is worse than none
- It exists to look prolific

## What long-form is for

It is not a longer post. Long-form earns its length by doing something a post cannot: making an
argument that needs its own evidence, or articulating a problem nobody has named yet.

**It is not:** a how-to anyone could write · received industry advice restated · a safe consensus
position · a promotional piece wearing an essay's clothes.

**It is:** an original position, backed by experience · a contrarian claim the user can defend · a
framework that gives the reader a new way to see a familiar problem · a prediction with the user's
own position attached · **the articulation of a problem the field has not yet put words to** — the
rarest and most valuable of the five.

## Five shapes

Pick the one that matches the argument. Forcing an argument into the wrong shape is why most
executive long-form reads as padded.

**Perspective** — the default
`The common belief → why it is incomplete → the user's position → the evidence → what follows from it → the new lens`

**Framework** — when the reader needs a way to think, not a conclusion
`The messy problem → why existing approaches fail → the model → its parts → how to apply it → what changes`

**Prediction** — the highest-risk shape, and the most memorable when right
`Where things stand → the signals the user is seeing → the call → roughly when → what it means → **what the user is doing about it**`

That last beat is what separates a prediction from punditry. A forecast with no personal exposure
costs the author nothing and reads that way.

**Lessons** — when the evidence is a scar
`The situation → the stakes → what went wrong → the insight → the generalizable principle → how to use it`

**State of the field** — annual or on a genuine inflection
`The picture → what is moving → what it means → who is positioned well → where it goes → what to do`

## Evidence, in order of weight

1. **The user's own experience** — the whole reason this piece is worth more than a search result
2. **Client or company outcomes**, within the confidentiality rules
3. **Industry data**, named and dated
4. **Historical parallel**
5. **Reasoning from first principles**
6. **Someone else's quote** — sparingly. A piece leaning on others' authority has none of its own

## Article structure

```
TITLE         Specific and searchable. It is the retrieval surface.
OPENING       The claim, in the first two sentences. No windup.
STAKES        Why this matters and to whom.
ARGUMENT      3-5 sections, each one point, each with evidence.
COUNTER       The strongest objection, answered honestly.
SO WHAT       What the reader should do or think differently.
```

The counterargument section is what separates an executive article from content marketing. An
argument that never concedes anything reads as advocacy, and senior readers discount it
automatically.

## Titles

Specific over clever. Titles are how a piece is found, cited, and retrieved.

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| Rethinking Attribution | Why Last-Touch Attribution Survives Every Attempt to Kill It |
| The Future of MarTech | What a 14-Tool Stack Actually Costs, in Hours and Dollars |
| Lessons in Leadership | What Broke When My Marketing Team Passed 20 People |

## Length by destination

| Format | Words |
|---|---|
| LinkedIn article | 800–1,500 |
| Newsletter essay | 1,000–2,500 |
| Owned-site post | 1,500–3,000 |

Length follows the argument. A 900-word piece that finishes its thought beats a 2,000-word piece
padded to look substantial.

## Newsletters

The only format that survives a platform change, because the list is owned.

- **Cadence over volume.** Monthly, sustained, beats weekly abandoned in six weeks. Recommend what
  the user will actually keep and say so when they propose otherwise
- **One idea per issue.** A newsletter that is a link roundup is a feed, not a point of view
- **Consistent shape.** Readers learn the structure and it lowers the cost of reading
- **Every issue is a Featured candidate** and a repurposing source for 3-4 posts

## Repurposing long-form down

One article supports weeks of posts, and this is the highest-leverage content move available.

1. Each section becomes a standalone post with its own hook
2. The counterargument section is often the best post — disagreement travels
3. The strongest data point becomes a single-number post
4. The conclusion becomes a point-of-view post
5. Link the full article in the first comment, never the body

Rebuild each one for the destination. Pasting a section is visible and reads as recycling.

## Evidence

Long-form claims face more scrutiny than posts, not less — the format signals authority and invites
checking. Every number traces to the ledger. Every external claim is attributed. Where the user is
speculating, the piece says so; speculation labeled as speculation is credible, and speculation
presented as finding is not.
