# Content Pillars

Three to five. Fewer than three and the feed repeats itself; more than five and nothing accumulates.

## A pillar is a topic plus an angle

This is the distinction that decides whether a content plan produces anything. A topic is what the
posts are about. An **angle** is what they say — and it is the part that makes the pillar the user's
rather than anyone's.

| Topic — not a pillar | Topic + angle — a pillar |
|---|---|
| Marketing measurement | Why most attribution problems turn out to be org-chart problems |
| Building marketing teams | What I keep changing about how I structure a team past twenty people |
| AI in marketing | Where AI has actually removed work for us, and where it has quietly added some |
| Leadership | The decisions I am least sure about this quarter |

The angle must be **defensible** — the user has to be able to argue it with someone who disagrees.
A pillar nobody could dispute is a category heading, and it will produce posts nobody remembers.

## Qualification

Each pillar must pass all five:

1. **Credible** — backed by facts in the ledger. Ask: what has the user actually done here?
2. **Wanted** — maps to a problem the target audience has
3. **Useful** — advances a role, a client, or standing in a category
4. **Durable** — two or three posts a month for a year without repeating or reaching
5. **Arguable** — someone credible could take the other side

Four and five are the ones that quietly kill content plans. A topic with three good posts in it
becomes visible strain by week five, and a pillar nobody could dispute produces posts nobody
remembers.

Failing any one makes it a hobby. Say so directly: *"You could write well about hiring, but nothing
in the record supports it and it doesn't move you toward a CMO seat. Worth doing anyway, just not
as a pillar."*

## Executive mix

| Share | Pillar | What it does | Reads it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 40-50% | Domain authority | Frameworks, teardowns, analysis in the core specialty | Peers, buyers, search partners |
| 20-30% | Leadership and judgment | How they decide, build, handle tradeoffs | Boards, CEOs, future teams |
| 15-25% | Point of view | Where they disagree with the field | Everyone; this is the memorable one |
| 10-15% | Human | Enough to read as a person | Everyone |

**The leadership pillar is the one most executives skip and the one that most distinguishes a
candidate for a senior seat.** Tactical content gets more reach; judgment content is what a board
member remembers three months later when a seat opens. Reach and consequence are different
objectives and this is where they diverge most sharply.

**The point-of-view pillar is the uncomfortable one.** It is also the differentiator. An executive
who never disagrees with anything in public has no discernible position. The disagreement must be
real, defensible, and specific — a manufactured contrarian take is worse than silence.

## Worked example

For a CMO targeting B2B SaaS operating roles:

| Pillar | Share | Backed by | Sample angles |
|---|---|---|---|
| Measurement and attribution | 45% | Attribution rebuild, MarTech consolidation | Why last-touch survives; what a real MMM costs; the $6M reallocation |
| Building marketing orgs | 25% | 22-person org across 3 units, 3 director promotions | Structuring around a funnel vs. a customer; when to hire senior; what breaks at 20 |
| Where marketing is wrong about AI | 20% | AI-assisted workflow rebuild | AI as work removal, not content volume; where it has not worked |
| The job itself | 10% | — | The parts nobody warns you about |

Note the fourth pillar has no ledger backing and is deliberately the smallest. Personal content does
not require evidence; it requires honesty, and it must stay a minority share or the profile stops
reading as an executive's.

## Anti-patterns

- **The everything pillar** — "marketing" is not a pillar
- **The trend pillar** — chasing whatever is current produces no accumulated authority
- **The aspiration pillar** — writing about what the user wants to be known for rather than what
  they have done. This is where invention creeps in
- **The vendor pillar** — content about the employer's product on a personal profile
- **Four pillars, one actually used** — a plan that is not kept is worse than a smaller one that is

## Rotation

Pillars are stable for six to twelve months. Changing them quarterly means none of them ever
accumulates authority, which is the entire point.

Retire a pillar when the goal changes, when the evidence stops supporting it, or when it
consistently produces no tier-1 or tier-2 outcomes over a quarter — see `salience-analytics`.
