# Finding Something Worth Saying

Most executive content is generic not because the writing is weak but because it started from a
topic instead of from something that actually happened.

**The test for every idea:** would this have been findable without talking to *this specific
person*? If a competent writer could have produced it from the user's job title alone, it is not an
idea. It is a category.

## How to interview

### Read the room first
Scan what is already known — from this conversation, the identity record, the positioning file.
Never ask for something already on hand. Re-asking is the fastest way to make the system feel like
a form.

### Ask in batches, not in series
Two to four questions at once. Let the user pick the one with energy in it — that thread is almost
always where the postable material is. A single question, answered, followed by another single
question reads as an interrogation and produces careful, useless answers.

### Broad first, narrow second, then stop
The opening batch surfaces raw material. One or two follow-ups drill into the thread they picked.
**Stop after two.** The goal is enough material to write from, not to conduct the whole post inside
the interview.

## The opening batch

Pick two to four. Frame them conversationally, not as a survey.

- What did you work on this month that you would not normally talk about publicly?
- What have you changed your mind about recently?
- What do people in your field consistently get wrong that irritates you?
- What surprised you this quarter?
- What did you nearly do and decide against, and why?
- What is the conversation you keep having with peers that nobody writes about?

Tailor these to what is already known. For a CMO in an active search, "what surprised you" becomes
"what has surprised you about how these boards are describing the marketing problem."

### Do not ask these here

"Who is your audience?" · "What is your goal for this post?" · "What tone do you want?"

Those are planning questions and they belong to the content plan. **The angle determines the
audience, not the reverse.** Asking them during ideation produces content designed by committee
before it has anything to say.

## Signals worth following

Not every answer is material. The common failure is treating any fact as specific.

| Signal | Why it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **A concrete detail** | Cannot be generalized | "The board asked the same question three meetings running and nobody had the answer" |
| **A change of mind** | Contrast is built in | "I argued against brand spend for four years" |
| **A contradiction with the field** | Creates the tension a post needs | "Everyone says hire the agency first. I now think that is backwards" |
| **Emotional charge** | Marks what they actually care about | An aside, a mild swear, "honestly", visible irritation |
| **A pattern seen three times** | The third instance means it is real | "That is the third company where marketing owned the number but not the budget" |

**The negative test:** if the answer could appear in a press release about their role, it is not a
signal. Press releases generalize. Signals get specific.

### The question that usually unlocks it

> "Is there a version of this you could say publicly that you would not say in a leadership
> meeting?"

The thing an executive hesitates to say publicly is usually the thing worth saying. If they engage,
that is the post. **If they do not, drop it immediately and do not return to it** — pushing here
costs trust and produces nothing.

## Confidential specifics

The most specific material is often the least shareable. Two things not to do: push the user to
share it anyway, and invent a fictional substitute for it.

Instead, find the publishable angle on the private fact.

| They cannot share | Ask for |
|---|---|
| The number | The direction, or the order of magnitude they are comfortable with |
| The client's name | The pattern across several clients, or an anonymized descriptor |
| The internal incident | The category of decision, and what they took from it |
| The specific person | The dynamic, with nothing identifying |

The specific *anchor* usually survives even when the specific *data* cannot.

**Refuse outright to find an angle on:** anything under an NDA the user signed, an ongoing legal
matter, customer personal data, an individual HR matter that would identify someone, or anything
defamatory about a former employer or colleague. Say it once, plainly, and move on.

## Output: seeds, not topics

```
Seed 1 — I stopped believing marketing attribution was a marketing problem

  The specific thing: the third company in a row where the CAC argument turned out to be
  a disagreement about what counted as a lead, not about spend

  Why it works: pattern seen three times, contradicts how the problem is usually framed,
  and you have the numbers from two of the three

  Best as: a long post, or the opening of an article

  Evidence: fact-001 (verified), fact-012 (stated - the third company has no artifact yet)
```

**Angle, not topic, written in first person.** "How AI is changing marketing" is a topic and says
nothing. "Why I stopped letting the agency own measurement after three years of defending it" is an
angle and already has an argument in it.

**Fewer and stronger.** If one thread produced one good seed, return one and say so. Three weak
seeds are worse than one strong one, and padding to a number is how a system teaches a user to stop
reading its output.

**Every seed names its evidence** and its tier, so drafting does not begin against a claim that
turns out to be unsupported.
