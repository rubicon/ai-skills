# Formats and Hooks

## Format selection

| Format | Length | Best for | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short post | 120-300 words | One sharp idea | Low. The workhorse |
| Long post | 700-1,300 chars | An argument with evidence | Medium |
| Framework / list | 700-1,300 chars | Saves and shares | Must be genuinely reusable |
| Teardown | 900-1,300 chars | Demonstrating method | Requires anonymization judgment |
| Case study | Long post or article | Proof | Requires client permission |
| Article | 800-2,000 words | Depth, durability, external citation | High effort, lower reach, longest half-life |
| Newsletter | Varies | Owned audience | Highest effort, only format that survives platform changes |
| Document / carousel | 6-10 slides | Frameworks, step sequences | Design time |
| Video script | 60-90 seconds spoken | Presence, and the format most people avoid | Recording time; highest trust return |

Match format to goal, not to what is performing. An executive who only writes what gets reach ends
up with a feed of tactics and no evidence of judgment.

## Hooks

The first line decides whether the rest is read. Roughly the first 210 characters show before the
fold on mobile.

**A hook must be true.** A hook that overstates to earn the click spends exactly the credibility the
content was written to build. This is the single most common failure in executive content, and the
audience notices immediately.

**Reveal the result, withhold the mechanism.** A hook should give away enough to be worth reading
and hold back the part that requires reading. "We cut CAC 34%" earns attention; "We cut CAC 34% by
rebuilding attribution first" has already finished and there is no reason to continue.

### Patterns that work

| Pattern | Shape | Example |
|---|---|---|
| The specific number | Lead with a real figure | "We found $6M going to channels taking credit for demand that already existed." |
| The defensible contrarian | State the disagreement plainly | "Most attribution problems are not attribution problems. They're org-chart problems." |
| The named mistake | Own something real | "I spent two quarters optimizing creative for a funnel whose measurement was broken." |
| The buyer's question | The thing they actually ask | "Every CEO I talk to asks the same thing: is this the market or is this us?" |
| Concrete before/after | The delta, stated flatly | "Fourteen tools to six. Reporting lag from three weeks to two days." |
| The concession | Give ground first | "The agency was right about the creative. They were wrong about why it wasn't working." |
| The unusual fact | Something hard to fabricate | "The attribution model we ran for four years was built by a contractor nobody could name." |
| The change of mind | The contradiction is the post | "I argued against brand spend for four years. I was wrong, and I can show you where." |

### Patterns that fail

- Manufactured suspense ("What happened next surprised me")
- "Most people don't know this…" / "Most people don't realize…"
- "Have you ever wondered…" / anything opening with "Imagine"
- "Let me tell you a story…"
- "Here's what I learned this week" / "Three lessons from my last role"
- A personal hardship repurposed as a business lesson
- Engagement bait with an obvious answer ("Agree?" / "Thoughts?")
- Opening with credentials ("As a CMO with 20 years…")
- A rhetorical question used as a formula
- Anything the body does not deliver on

**Order the variants strongest to weakest, name the strongest, and say why.** An unranked list of
five hooks is the system declining to have an opinion.

## Video scripts

The format most executives avoid, and the one that builds trust fastest — a face and a voice are
harder to fake than prose, and at senior level the audience is evaluating judgment, which is more
legible in speech.

```
OPEN       One sentence. The claim, no throat-clearing. No "Hi, I'm..." — the name is on screen.
STAKES     Why this matters, in one line.
BODY       Two or three points. One idea each. Speak them, don't read them.
CLOSE      The thought you want left behind.
```

**Write in beats, not paragraphs.** One claim per beat, one or two short sentences, then a breath.
Long sentences fail on camera — the speaker runs out of air and the viewer loses the thread.

Rough budget for 60 seconds, at roughly 140–160 spoken words:

| Section | Time | Words |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | 0–3s | ~15 |
| Why it matters | 3–15s | ~30 |
| Substance — 2 to 4 beats | 15–50s | 80–100 |
| Close | 50–60s | 15–20 |

For 45 seconds, cut a beat. For 90, add one or develop an existing one.

- **60-90 seconds.** Past that, completion drops sharply
- **Write for speech, not for reading.** Short clauses, contractions, one idea per breath
- **Captions always** — most viewing is muted
- **The first three seconds decide whether it plays.** No intro card, no logo animation
- Read it aloud before delivering. Anything that catches in the mouth gets rewritten

- **No wordplay that only works aloud.** Much of the audience is reading captions
- **End on a cue.** A takeaway or a question. A cold cut reads as the speaker forgetting their line

Do not script filler ("Let me know your thoughts below"). Do not write stage directions the user
will read out by accident.

## Post structure

```
HOOK          1-2 lines. Survives the fold. True.
CONTEXT       1-2 lines. Why this matters to the reader.
BODY          The argument. Short paragraphs, whitespace between.
EVIDENCE      The specific thing that makes it credible. From the ledger.
CLOSE         One thought or one question. Not a CTA on every post.
```

**Argument shape.** The most reliable structure for a short argument is *and, but, therefore* —
context the reader accepts, the complication that breaks it, the consequence that follows. Most
flat posts are missing the "but".

**Active voice.** If you can append "by zombies" to a clause and it still reads, rewrite it.
"The stack was consolidated" fails. "We consolidated the stack" passes.

**Whitespace is structural on LinkedIn.** Unbroken text renders as a grey slab on mobile and is
skipped regardless of quality.

**Links go in the first comment.** Links in the body suppress distribution.

**Not every post needs a CTA.** An executive whose every post ends in an ask reads as selling. Most
posts should end on a thought.

## Length

- Under 700 characters: usually underdeveloped for an argument
- 700-1,300: the working range for a substantive post
- Over 1,300: consider an article — the format signals depth and lasts longer

Length is a consequence of the idea, not a target. A 300-character post that says one true thing
well is better than a padded 1,200-character version of it.

## Scoring

Before delivery:

| Dimension | Fails when |
|---|---|
| Hook | Does not survive the fold, or overstates |
| Specificity | A peer could have written it verbatim |
| Evidence | A claim the ledger does not support |
| Voice | Does not match the profile |
| Audience fit | Written for peers when the target is buyers |
| Close | No thought or action left with the reader |

Report honestly with one specific improvement. A 4/10 told it is a 7 gets published, underperforms,
and teaches nothing.
