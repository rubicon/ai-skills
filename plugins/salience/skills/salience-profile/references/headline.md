# Headline

220 characters. The single highest-leverage field on the profile: it follows the user into every
search result, comment, connection request, and notification. It is also the only field most people
ever see.

Use close to all 220 characters. A 60-character headline forfeits index surface and reads as an
unfinished profile.

## Base formula

```
[What you do] | [Who you help, specifically] | [The result, with proof where it exists]
```

Each segment does one job. Lead with value, not title — the title is already rendered directly
above the headline, so repeating it wastes the most valuable characters on the profile.

## Three strategic variants

Always produce three, differentiated by **strategy**, not by synonym choice. Three headlines that
differ only in wording are one headline.

### A — Authority-forward
Leads with the role and the outcome it creates. Safest in an active search, because search partners
scan for role nouns.

```
Marketing executive for B2B SaaS | Built the AI-native demand engine at Hollis — 34% lower CAC, 3.4x pipeline | CMO / VP Marketing
```

### B — Outcome-forward
Leads with the result; identity is secondary. Strongest with founders and operating partners, who
buy outcomes before credentials.

```
I take B2B SaaS marketing from stalled to compounding — 34% lower CAC in 11 months | Brand, demand, and MarTech under one P&L | Fractional CMO
```

### C — Category-claiming
Owns a specific position no peer can copy verbatim. Highest ceiling, highest risk: it must be
defensible or it reads as bluster.

```
The CMO for B2B SaaS companies whose growth stalled when the old playbook stopped working | AI-enabled demand, MarTech, and brand | Advisory + fractional
```

### Recommend one, and say what it costs

Name which to run first, in two or three sentences: which goal it serves, **who it will deliberately
repel**, and what to watch over the next 30 days. A headline that attracts everyone attracts nobody;
naming the exclusion is the point.

Flag it when C rests on a claim the fact ledger does not support. Write it anyway — then say plainly
that it is the strongest and least defensible option, and offer to build the proof or drop it. That
is the user's call, not yours.

## Keyword placement

LinkedIn indexes the headline heavily, and recruiter search is largely a keyword match against a
title vocabulary.

**Position matters as much as presence.** The opening of the headline carries the most search
weight — put the searchable role noun in roughly the **first 50 characters**, not at the end after
a value claim. Everything past that is read by humans and does comparatively little for discovery.

This is the one place where the "lead with value, not title" rule needs care: lead with value, but
get the role noun in early. "Marketing executive for B2B SaaS | Built the demand engine at Hollis"
puts both in the first 50. "Built the demand engine that took CAC down 34% | CMO" buries the noun
past the indexing window and loses recruiter search for a rhetorical gain.

Include, naturally:
- **The target role noun, in the words recruiters actually type** — "CMO", "VP Marketing", "Head of
  Marketing". Include the spelled-out and abbreviated forms of the primary target when both are
  commonly searched, but only once each; "CMO | Chief Marketing Officer | Marketing Executive |
  Marketing Leader" is keyword stuffing and reads as desperation.
- **The industry or company profile** — "B2B SaaS", "FinTech", "PE-backed"
- **The differentiating specialty** — "MarTech", "marketing automation", "AI-enabled marketing",
  "demand generation"

Do not include: the current employer's name unless it carries transferable weight, degrees,
certifications with no search value, or a location.

## For someone open to more than one path

An executive pursuing full-time roles *and* consulting has a real conflict: the two audiences want
different signals. Resolve it explicitly rather than blurring.

- **Both, sequentially:** lead with the full-time framing, close with the advisory tail
  (`... | CMO / VP Marketing · Advisory & fractional`). Costs a little authority in each direction
  and keeps both doors open.
- **Both, cleanly:** pick the primary for the headline and carry the secondary in About and
  Featured, where there is room to explain.

Ask which the user wants. Do not silently optimize for one.

## Anti-patterns

| Pattern | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| "Open to Work" in the headline | Broadcasts availability to clients and boards; belongs in the setting, which reaches recruiters without the cost |
| A bare title | The title is already displayed above; this wastes the field entirely |
| Every title ever held, pipe-separated | Reads as unfocused; dilutes every keyword |
| "Passionate", "driven", "results-oriented" | Zero signal, consumes characters, appears on millions of profiles |
| "20+ years of experience" | Nobody searches it, and at executive level it can invite age filtering without adding information |
| Emoji chains | Read as low-effort to the exact audience being targeted |
| ALL CAPS | Reads as shouting; no index benefit |

## Executive note

Seniority is conveyed by **scope and outcome**, not by adjectives. "Transformational leader" claims
seniority; "Rebuilt marketing across 3 business units, $40M budget, 60 people" demonstrates it and
survives a reference call. Prefer the second in every case where a fact exists to support it.
