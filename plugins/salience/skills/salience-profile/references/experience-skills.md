# Experience, Skills, Custom URL, Recommendations

## Experience

### Formula

```
[Strong action verb] + [what changed] + [specific metric] + [over what period, where it matters]
```

Not "Responsible for" or "Managed" — those describe a job description rather than a person, and
they tell a search partner nothing about scope or outcome.

### Executive roles need a scope line

This is the difference between a senior profile and a manager's profile with bigger numbers. Open
each executive role with one sentence establishing scope before the bullets:

```
Hollis Data · Chief Marketing Officer · 2023–present
Marketing across 3 business units — 22 people, $40M media budget, full demand and brand P&L.

• Rebuilt acquisition around signal over volume: blended CAC down 34% in 11 months
• Grew qualified pipeline 3.4x while holding headcount flat
• Consolidated 14 MarTech tools to 6, cutting $1.1M annual spend and 3 weeks of reporting lag
```

A search partner validates scope first and outcomes second. A profile that lists achievements
without scope reads as ambiguous seniority, which is worse than modest seniority.

**Three things the scope line should carry, and most profiles carry none of them:**

1. **Company context**, not just personal scope — "$180M B2B SaaS, 900 people, PE-backed". A search
   partner is calibrating the size of the job, and "led 22 people" means something different at a
   $20M company than at a $2B one.
2. **The mandate.** Why this person was brought in. "Recruited by the CEO to rebuild demand after
   two flat years" is worth more than any bullet beneath it, because it frames everything that
   follows as against-a-brief rather than routine.
3. **Span of control, named.** "Direct reports: demand gen, brand, marketing ops, product
   marketing" is concrete where "led the marketing org" is not.

### The transformation shape

Executives are hired to change something, not to hold it steady. Where the facts support it, shape
the role around:

> **Inherited** [the situation]. **Did** [the specific change]. **Ended with** [the outcome].

That shape answers the only question a hiring board actually has, which is what happens when this
person arrives.

### Before → after

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Managed the marketing team | Led 22 marketers across brand, demand, and ops; 3 promoted into director roles |
| Responsible for marketing technology | Consolidated 14 tools to 6; $1.1M saved annually, reporting lag cut from 3 weeks to 2 days |
| Oversaw demand generation | Grew qualified pipeline 3.4x in 11 months while holding budget flat |
| Worked with sales leadership | Built the joint pipeline model with the CRO; forecast accuracy from 61% to 89% |
| Led digital transformation | Replaced last-touch attribution with a mixed-media model; reallocated $6M off channels that were taking credit for organic demand |

### Verbs

**Use:** Led · Built · Rebuilt · Grew · Cut · Shipped · Launched · Closed · Consolidated · Scaled ·
Restructured · Turned around · Recruited · Established

**Avoid:** Responsible for · Managed · Oversaw · Handled · Assisted with · Participated in ·
Helped to · Was part of · Worked on

"Managed" is the specific trap at executive level — it is technically accurate and conveys
custodianship rather than change.

### Attribution discipline

Distinguish what the user did personally from what their team did under their leadership. Both are
legitimate executive claims and they are worded differently:

- Personal: "Rebuilt the attribution model"
- Led team: "Led the team that rebuilt the attribution model"

At CMO level, leading is the job — "led the team that" is not a weaker claim, it is the accurate
one, and a reference call will make the distinction whether or not the profile did.

Equally: outcomes *caused* versus outcomes *coincident with tenure*. "Revenue grew 40% while I led
marketing" is defensible when marketing was one contributor. "I grew revenue 40%" may not be.

### Confidential employer information

Executives routinely hold numbers they cannot publish — private-company revenue, margins,
headcount, undisclosed transactions. Publishing these is a genuine professional and sometimes
contractual problem, and it is not covered by "it's my own achievement."

| Situation | Publish as |
|---|---|
| Private-company revenue or margin | The percentage change, not the absolute — "grew revenue 3.2x" not "grew revenue to $180M" |
| Headcount or budget under NDA | A band — "a team of 20+", "an eight-figure budget" |
| An undisclosed acquisition or transaction | The nature of the work, never the counterparty — "led marketing diligence on an acquisition" |
| A metric the company has published | Freely, and cite where it was published |
| Proprietary method or strategy | The outcome, never the mechanism |

Never name a confidential acquisition target, never publish an undisclosed financial, and never
describe a strategy a former employer would recognize as theirs. When in doubt, publish the
percentage and record a gap — and flag it, because this is one of the few places where the honest
version is also the legally safer one.

### Length and media

- 3–6 bullets for current and recent roles; 2–3 for roles older than about ten years
- Roles beyond ~15 years back can compress to title, company, dates
- Attach media where it genuinely exists — a talk, a case study, a published piece. Do not
  manufacture an artifact to fill the slot

### Skills per role

Tag at least 5 skills per role. This is a search-index surface: skills attached to a role carry
more weight than skills floating in the Skills section alone.

## Skills

- **Quality over count.** The cap is 50, but filling it is not the goal — a profile padded with
  weak, unendorsed skills dilutes the strong ones and reads as unfocused to the human who opens it
  after the search returns it. Roughly 15–25 genuine, relevant skills is the working range for an
  executive. List a skill because it is real and relevant, never to use a slot.
- **Pin the top 3 deliberately.** These render first and should match the headline claim exactly.
  If the headline says AI-enabled demand generation and the pinned skills are "Leadership,
  Communication, Teamwork", the profile is arguing with itself.
- **Mirror the target vocabulary.** In an active search, mirror the language of the job descriptions
  being targeted — recruiters search the terms their postings use.
- **Endorsements matter for indexing.** A skill with zero endorsements carries little search weight.
  Prioritize getting at least one on the pinned three.
- **Remove stale skills.** Anything unused in 3+ years is noise that dilutes the signal.

**Executive skill set** — pair leadership terms with functional and technical ones. A profile listing
only "Leadership, Strategy, Management" is unsearchable, because nobody runs that search. Pair them
with: Demand Generation · Marketing Technology · Marketing Automation · Marketing Analytics ·
Brand Strategy · Performance Marketing · Product Marketing · Marketing Operations · P&L Management ·
Go-to-Market Strategy · AI-Enabled Marketing.

Do not list a skill the user cannot discuss for ten minutes under questioning.

## Custom URL

`linkedin.com/in/firstnamelastname` — never the default hash tail.

Profile → Edit public profile & URL → Edit custom URL. Under a minute, and it improves how the
profile resolves in search engines, in email signatures, and on a résumé header.

If the exact name is taken, prefer `firstnamelastname` plus a role word over adding digits.
`/in/morganreyes-cmo` reads deliberate; `/in/morgan-reyes-8823a41` reads like an unfinished profile.

## Recommendations

Target 3 or more, recent, from varied contexts: someone senior, a peer, a direct report, a client.
Range matters more than volume — five recommendations from one team says less than three from four
different vantage points.

### How to ask

Never use the LinkedIn default request. Ask directly, name the specifics, and offer to draft.

```
Hi [Name],

I'm refreshing my LinkedIn and would value a recommendation from you.

If you're open to it, the parts most useful to me are:
- [the specific project or period you worked together]
- [the concrete outcome]
- [the capability you'd want a future employer or client to know about]

Happy to draft two or three bullets you can edit or ignore — should take you about a minute
either way. And glad to write yours first if that's useful.
```

### Rules

- **Reciprocate first** where possible. Writing theirs first roughly doubles the response rate and
  is the right thing to do regardless.
- **Never draft a recommendation as the recommender and present it as theirs.** Offering bullets a
  human then edits and submits is normal professional courtesy. Writing the recommendation and
  having someone paste it is fabricated social proof, and Salience will not produce it.
- **Ask soon after a visible win,** not during a job search — a burst of new recommendations dated
  the same week reads exactly like what it is.
- **After a role change,** ask one or two from the previous role. Not five.
