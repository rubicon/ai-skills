# Executive Narrative

The story that explains the arc. It runs under every interview answer, About section, and
introduction, and it must be true, brief, and forward-facing.

## Three sentences

1. **The arc.** What the through-line has been — the capability that compounded across roles.
2. **The move.** What this next step is toward.
3. **Why now.** What makes this the moment.

```
I've spent fifteen years on the measurement side of marketing - the part that tells you
whether any of the rest worked - and kept ending up running the whole function because
that's where the answers live. Hollis was the clearest version: fix attribution, then
rebuild demand against what it says. I want to do that at a larger scale, ideally
somewhere PE-backed where the board actually wants the number rather than the narrative.
```

## Rules

- **Forward-facing.** The story explains where someone is going, not where they have been.
- **Never critical of a former employer.** Criticism of the last place is read as a preview of what
  will be said about the next one. This holds even when the criticism is fair.
- **Brief.** Length signals defensiveness. A three-sentence version and a ninety-second version;
  nothing longer.
- **True.** A narrative that smooths a real discontinuity into a clean arc will meet a reference
  call that remembers it differently.

## Discontinuities

Every senior career has them. Handle plainly.

### A gap
State it in one clause, then move to what came of it. The evasive version is always more damaging
than the fact.

> "I took nine months off after the acquisition closed — my father was ill and it was the right
> call. I spent the back half of it advising two portfolio companies, which is where the fractional
> work started."

### A short tenure
Name it before it is asked about. Give the reason once, without litigating it.

> "Eleven months at Vector. The mandate I was hired for was cancelled two quarters in when they
> pivoted to enterprise. I stayed to land the transition and left when it was clean."

### A layoff
Say the word. Euphemism reads as shame, and at executive level layoffs are routine and understood.

> "The whole marketing org went in the restructuring — 40% of the company."

### A long tenure
Twelve years at one company reads as either deep commitment or as someone who stopped moving. The
distinguishing evidence is **reinvention**: show progression through roles and the distinct problems
solved in each, not one long undifferentiated block.

> "Four roles over eleven years. I joined to run demand gen for a $40M company and left running
> marketing for a $400M one. The job changed underneath me three times."

### A step back in title
Frame around scope or situation, not status.

> "Smaller title, twice the scope. I'd rather own a P&L at a $60M company than run a function at a
> $2B one."

### A pivot
Lead with the through-line, not the change. The capability that transferred is the story.

## Employed and exploring

Real constraints: discretion, loyalty, and the fact that current colleagues read the profile.

- Position around **capability and point of view**, never availability
- Never imply dissatisfaction with the current employer, publicly or in a first conversation
- Signal direction through subject matter, not declared intent — an executive writing consistently
  about PE-backed turnarounds is understood without saying anything
- Availability lives in the recruiter-only setting, not in public copy
- Do not publish a burst of new recommendations and a rewritten profile in the same week; the
  pattern is legible

## Age and currency

When level or currency concerns surface obliquely ("this is a fast-moving space", "very hands-on
team"), answer the real question — currency, energy, adaptability — with evidence rather than
reassurance.

> "I rebuilt our stack around AI-assisted workflows last year, mostly because I got tired of
> waiting for the team to have capacity. I still write the SQL when a question matters enough."

Never defensive, never apologetic, and never a claim that could not be demonstrated in the next
ten minutes.
