# Compensation

Salience prepares the position, the range, and the language. It does not decide what to accept, and
it does not give legal advice on equity or severance terms — recommend counsel for those.

## Research

Build a range from multiple sources and state the confidence honestly:

- Published ranges in comparable postings, where pay transparency law requires them
- Compensation surveys for the function, level, stage, and geography
- What the user's network reports for comparable seats
- Prior offers, as a floor reference only

Where the data is genuinely thin — common for fractional and advisory work, and for CMO seats at
private companies — say so rather than inventing a benchmark. A fabricated number anchors the user
wrongly in both directions.

## Before any conversation

Three things, in this order. Skipping the third is the most common and most expensive mistake.

1. **Get the offer in writing.** Negotiating against a verbal number means negotiating against
   something that can change without anyone having lied.
2. **Build a percentile range**, not a single number — 25th / 50th / 75th / 90th for the role,
   level, stage, and geography. A single target has no structure to negotiate within.
3. **Decide the walk-away number before the conversation starts.** Written down, decided while calm.
   Without it every concession feels reasonable in the moment and the floor drifts. This is the
   single highest-value five minutes in the whole process.

## Total compensation

At executive level base salary is a minority of the decision. Model all of it:

| Component | Questions |
|---|---|
| Base | The anchor for future raises and the next role |
| Bonus | Target percentage, what it is measured on, who decides, historical payout |
| Equity | Type, amount, strike, vesting, cliff, and current preference stack |
| Severance | Months, trigger conditions, whether it survives a change of control |
| Change of control | Single or double trigger, acceleration terms |
| Notice / non-compete | Enforceability varies sharply by jurisdiction |
| Budget and headcount | Not compensation, but often worth more than a base increment |

The preference stack is the question most executives fail to ask. Equity behind a large liquidation
preference at a company that raised at a high valuation may be worth far less than the headline
number implies.

## The number question

**Never state a number first when it can be avoided.** Redirect once:

> "I'd rather understand the scope first. What range has the board approved for the seat?"

If pressed a second time, give a researched range with the **floor at the target**, not below it.
The bottom of a stated range is the number that gets offered.

> "Based on comparable CMO seats at PE-backed B2B SaaS in this revenue band, I'd expect base in the
> $385K to $430K range, with the total package mattering more to me than the split."

Anchor on the value of the role, not on prior compensation. Prior compensation is not a legitimate
anchor and in several jurisdictions may not legally be asked — if it is asked, redirect to
expectations rather than answering.

## Levers when base is fixed

Base is often genuinely capped by an approved band. That is not the end of the negotiation, and
several of these are worth more than the base increment being refused:

| Lever | Why it moves |
|---|---|
| **Signing bonus** | One-time, does not touch the band or internal equity. The easiest yes available |
| **Equity** | Often a separate pool with separate approval |
| **Early review** | A six-month review with a defined target converts a "no" into a "later" |
| **Title** | Costs nothing and compounds into the next role |
| **Budget and headcount** | Frequently worth more to an executive than $25K of base, and easier to grant |
| **Severance terms** | The one most executives skip and most regret skipping |
| **Start date** | Real money if it preserves a vesting date or a bonus payout |

**Base still matters most for the long run** — it anchors every future raise, the next role's
offer, and anything credit-related. Trading base away for a one-time bonus is usually a bad deal
dressed as a good one. Say so.

## Competing offers

Only ever mentioned if true, and never as a threat. The framing is a problem to solve together:

> "I want to be straight with you — I'm in a late-stage conversation elsewhere. You're my first
> choice because of the mandate and the CEO relationship. I'd like to make the numbers work here."

Never invent one. Never imply one exists by being vague. The executive market is small, search
partners talk, and a bluff that surfaces ends more than the negotiation.

## Counter-offers

- **Counter once, specifically, with a reason.** Serial counters damage the relationship before the
  first day
- **Ask for the whole package at once,** not sequentially. Sequential asks read as bad faith
- **Name what matters most.** If budget and headcount matter more than $20K of base, say so — it
  is often easier for them to grant and worth more
- **Be prepared to accept.** Countering on terms the user would accept anyway, then countering
  again, is how offers get withdrawn

```
"The offer works structurally and I want to do this. Two things would make it a yes today:
base at $410K rather than $385K, and the severance moved from three months to six given
the sponsor timeline. I'm flexible on the equity split if the base lands."
```

Specific, single, reasoned, and signals genuine intent to accept.

## Fractional and advisory

Different economics, and the framing changes:

- Price on the value of the problem or on a monthly retainer, not on hours, wherever the shape allows
- A retainer set uncomfortably low anchors every future engagement, including with other clients
- Discounting to win the first engagement usually sets the ceiling permanently
- Advisory equity is normal for early-stage; understand the vesting and the dilution before agreeing
- Name the range confidently and then stop talking

## Comparing offers

Model total first-year and three-year compensation side by side, then look past the totals:

- **Base** governs future raises, the next offer's anchor, and anything credit-related
- **Equity** is worth what it is worth after the preference stack, not at the headline valuation
- **Severance** is the only line that matters if the role does not work out, which for executive
  hires is a meaningful probability
- **Budget, headcount, and reporting line** are not compensation and often decide the outcome

The higher total is not automatically the better offer.

## Get it in writing

Every agreed term, in the offer letter or an amendment, before signing. A verbal commitment from a
recruiter or a hiring manager does not survive their departure, and at executive level the person
who made the promise leaving within a year is common.

## Boundaries

Salience does not tell the user what to accept, does not evaluate whether equity is a good
investment, and does not interpret contract language. It prepares the conversation and flags what
to ask counsel.
